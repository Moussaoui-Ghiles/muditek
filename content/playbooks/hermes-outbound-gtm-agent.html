The agent is not the sales process

An AI agent does not learn how your company sells from one prompt.

A mature sales process contains years of decisions: which companies are worth pursuing, which buyers own the problem, which accounts must be excluded, what qualifies as a real buying signal, which customer examples apply, and which claims the company can defend.

If your ICP, offer, exclusions, qualification logic, customer examples, and approved claims only live in your head, Hermes has to recreate the strategy for every account. That is where plausible-looking research and invented pain enter the workflow.

Do not ask the agent to invent the sales process. Put the sales process into rules, records, and examples the agent can follow.

This playbook does exactly that. It gives Hermes a defined market, a fixed evidence bar, a 100-point scoring model, explicit approvals, and a CRM state that every tool must obey.

1. Connect the stack you already use

Hermes does not replace your sales tools. It moves the work between them and makes the decision required at each handoff.

ToolOne job in the workflow
ApolloFind accounts and buyers that match the market definition.
ClayEnrich records and run providers in a controlled order.
CrustdataRetrieve current company, hiring, job, web, and social evidence.
HermesResearch, qualify, score, choose the next state, and prepare the message.
TelegramShow one approval queue with the account, evidence, score, signal, and proposed actions.

UnipileExecute approved LinkedIn actions.
HubSpotHold the current state and record every outcome.

You can replace any provider. The pattern remains:

market → research → signal → message → approval → action → CRM

The manual handoffs disappear. Your judgment about the market, the offer, and which actions require permission does not.

For the first version, keep Smartlead and Unipile off. Build the research and approval queue first. No prospect should be contacted while you are still checking whether the system chooses the right accounts, buyers, signals, and evidence.

2. Define who can move forward

“Find growing SaaS companies that need more pipeline” is not an instruction. It pushes every important decision onto the agent.

Hermes would still have to decide what “growing” means, which companies count as SaaS, who owns pipeline, which countries are allowed, and what evidence proves a need. Define those decisions before discovery begins.

Write four things:

  1. The companies you want.
  2. The buyers you want inside those companies.
  3. The accounts and contacts Hermes must exclude.
  4. The fields that must be present before outreach can be proposed.

Market and buyer rules

account:
  industries: [b2b_saas, developer_tools]
  employee_range: [50, 500]
  countries: [United States, United Kingdom, Ireland,
              Norway, Sweden, Denmark]
  funding_stages: [series_a, series_b, series_c,
                   bootstrapped_profitable]

buyer:
  functions: [sales, revenue_operations, growth]
  titles: [VP Sales, Head of Sales, Chief Revenue Officer,
           VP Revenue Operations]

exclude:
  [existing_customer, active_opportunity, direct_competitor,
   agency, suppressed_contact]

required_before_outreach:
  [company_domain, named_buyer, current_signal,
   source, retrieval_date]

Run exclusions first. An existing customer or active opportunity must not re-enter cold outbound because the rest of the account looks attractive. The same rule applies to competitors, suppressed contacts, agencies, and excluded markets.

Email adds one more gate: a verified address. An account can qualify for research or LinkedIn without one. It cannot enter an email sequence.

Worked example: Linear

Public company information tells us what Linear does and identifies a senior sales buyer. That supports the company-fit and buyer fields. It does not give us a reason to contact the company now. The account still needs a current signal.

3. Turn a large market into a short review queue

The first real job is not “send outreach.” It is “reduce a large market to the few accounts worth reviewing.”

  1. Apollo discovers the market. Apply the company size, industry, location, technology, job title, and seniority filters from the rules.
  2. Clay fills the gaps. Run cheaper checks first and skip expensive enrichment when an account already fails.
  3. Crustdata checks what is current. Retrieve fresh company, hiring, job, web, and social evidence instead of relying on a stale company profile.
  4. Hermes researches and scores. Turn the inputs into one review record.
  5. HubSpot stores the result. The record now has one state and one next action.

Keep discovery separate from contactability. Finding the right Head of Sales does not mean you have a verified email address for that person.

Five questions every account must answer

  • Why does this company fit?
  • Who is the likely buyer?
  • What changed recently?
  • Which claims can we support?
  • What should Hermes do next?

If any answer is missing, the account stays out of outreach.

The 100-point decision

25Company fit
20Observable problem
20Recent signal
15Correct buyer
10Offer relevance
10Evidence quality

Every point needs a reason. Every reason needs a source and retrieval date.

Evidence record

claim: Linear launched Loops for Business and Enterprise customers.
source: company product announcement
retrieved_at: 2026-08-09
confidence: high

Before the timing signal counts, Linear scores 64 out of 100. The company fits, the buyer fits, and the source is strong. The observable-problem score stays low because a public product launch does not prove a private pipeline problem.

“We do not know” is a valid research result. A public event can explain timing. It does not automatically prove pain.

4. Wait for a reason to act now

A company can fit your ICP and still give you no reason to contact it this week.

Qualified accounts enter monitoring. They move forward only when a current event makes the offer relevant.

Useful signals include:

  • a funding round;
  • a new executive in the buying function;
  • a product or segment launch;
  • geographic expansion;
  • relevant hiring or a technology change;
  • a public company priority or competitor change;
  • a new compliance requirement or relevant integration.

Every signal needs five fields:

Signal record

event: what happened
date: when it happened
relevance: why it matters to this offer
source: where the information came from
expiry: when the reason becomes stale

For Linear, the signal is the Loops launch. It is recent, relevant to the example offer, and tied to Business and Enterprise customers. It adds the 20 timing points and moves Linear from 64 to 84.

Hermes checks monitored accounts on a schedule. If it finds an approved signal, the account moves forward. If nothing has changed, the account waits.

find → qualify → monitor → signal → research → review

Signals expire. A launch from last month may still justify outreach. The same announcement next year probably does not.

5. Write only from approved evidence

Give the writing step the approved account record—not every scrap collected during enrichment.

Every sentence must be one of four types:

  1. Fact. A source directly supports it.
  2. Inference. A cautious interpretation using language such as “may” or “might.”
  3. Offer claim. Something your company can genuinely support.
  4. Question. A simple next step that does not assume interest.

The sentence to reject

“Your sales team is probably scrambling to turn Loops into enterprise pipeline.”

The source says Linear launched Loops. It does not say the sales team is scrambling, pipeline is the problem, or the company wants outside help.

The evidence-bound version

Linear outreach draft

Subject: 10 accounts for the Loops launch

Casey, the Loops launch creates a pretty specific outbound angle:
enterprise teams already talking publicly about governed or
scheduled agent workflows.

I've been building a system that finds those accounts, maps the
right buyer, and prepares outreach around the signal.

Would it be useful if I mapped the first 10 for Linear?

The first sentence is fact plus cautious relevance. The second is the supported offer. The last is the question. If Hermes cannot classify a sentence into one of the four types, remove it.

6. Approve each channel separately

Telegram is not a feed of everything Hermes found. It is a short queue of accounts that cleared the rules and now need a decision.

Each review card shows:

  • company, buyer, score, and current signal;
  • the sources and retrieval dates behind the research;
  • the email draft and verified destination, if one exists;
  • the LinkedIn draft and profile;
  • a separate approval control for each channel.

Email and LinkedIn are two separate switches. Approving email unlocks Smartlead. Approving LinkedIn unlocks Unipile. If the draft changes, that channel locks until the new version is approved.

In the Linear example, email remains unavailable because there is no verified address. LinkedIn receives a simulated approval. Only LinkedIn can move.

Increase autonomy one proven decision at a time

  • Automate research after account selection stays reliable.
  • Automate monitoring after stale signals expire correctly.
  • Automate low-risk actions only after the review rules survive enough real examples.
  • Keep high-risk external actions behind explicit permission.

Increase autonomy or volume. Never increase both in the same change.

7. Make HubSpot control the state

Smartlead and Unipile execute actions. HubSpot decides whether those actions are still allowed.

discovered → qualified → monitoring → signal found → researched → pending review → approved → active → replied → resolved

Every provider event has one outcome:

EventSystem response
Approved sequence startsMark only that channel active.
Any reply arrivesPause every active channel before classification.
Unsubscribe arrivesSuppress the contact globally.
Duplicate event arrivesIgnore it.
Provider keeps failingCreate a task and leave the current state unchanged.
Provider and HubSpot disagreeStop. The CRM state wins until a person resolves the conflict.

If Smartlead says active while HubSpot says replied, stop. If Unipile says connected while HubSpot says suppressed, stop. One sending tool must never revive a lead the CRM has already stopped.

Before live execution, add daily caps, destination checks, duplicate prevention, signal expiry, webhook deduplication, and global unsubscribe handling.

LinkedIn carries platform and account risk. An integration does not remove LinkedIn’s rules. Review the current terms before enabling automated actions.

8. Stop every channel on any reply

Pause first. Classify second.

Do not ask a model whether a reply is important enough to stop the sequence while another tool keeps sending. Any reply pauses every active channel immediately.

Once outreach is paused, Hermes can classify the response:

  • positive, objection, referral, or not now;
  • not interested or unsubscribe;
  • automatic reply, wrong person, or ambiguous.

Hermes then records the classification in HubSpot, alerts the owner, creates the appropriate task, and prepares a suggested response. It does not send the substantive sales reply.

In the Linear simulation, a positive response pauses LinkedIn, confirms email never started, moves HubSpot to replied, and creates a task for the account owner.

Once a prospect engages, the outbound workflow has completed its job. A person owns the sales conversation from that point.

9. Improve the rules from results

Hermes can surface the pattern. It cannot change the playbook without a reviewable case.

Each week, break results down by segment, signal, buyer, channel, and message angle. Show which accounts were approved, which messages were rewritten, and which signals produced real conversations.

Track the protection metrics too: bounces, unsubscribes, duplicate contacts, provider failures, and sequences stopped after replies. Every rate needs its denominator. Every recommendation needs the accounts and messages behind it.

Hermes can propose a change to a signal window, score weight, exclusion, buyer, message angle, or experiment. Every proposal must include:

  1. what happened;
  2. which records support the change;
  3. the expected effect;
  4. what could go wrong;
  5. how the change will be tested;
  6. the condition that triggers rollback.

A person reviews that case and decides whether the playbook changes.

results → proposal → test → approval → new rule

10. Bring the system online

Prove the decisions on one narrow segment before you add volume.

  • Install the Hermes skill and keep every execution tool in test mode.
  • Write the ICP, buyer roles, exclusions, required fields before outreach, and approved offer claims.
  • Connect account discovery, enrichment, and current-signal sources.
  • Create the 100-point scoring rules and the list of approved signals.
  • Create HubSpot fields for research, approval, sequence, reply, and suppression.
  • Connect Telegram or another review interface.
  • Generate a review queue for one narrow market.
  • Review every account, buyer, score, signal, source, and message in the first batch.
  • Add monitoring and confirm old events expire.
  • Connect email and LinkedIn in test mode.
  • Trigger reply, bounce, unsubscribe, duplicate, stale-signal, and provider-failure scenarios.
  • Go live with a small daily cap and keep the approval queue in place.

Start with 20 to 25 accounts from one segment.

Do not judge the system by the number of messages it produces. Judge it by how often you agree with five decisions: the account, buyer, timing, evidence, and proposed action.

When those decisions stay reliable, increase autonomy or volume. Do not change both at once.

target → find → research → wait → write → approve → send → stop → learn

That is what turns Hermes from a writing assistant into an outbound operator: it keeps the pipeline moving between decisions, preserves the evidence behind every action, and hands the conversation to a person the moment the prospect engages.
