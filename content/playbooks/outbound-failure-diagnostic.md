# The Hidden Reason Your B2B Outbound Is Not Working, and the Exact Plan, Numbers and AI Agent Skills to Fix It

You tried outbound because you needed more qualified leads.

Instead, the campaign generated activity. Messages went out. Lists were worked. Perhaps replies or meetings appeared. But too little of that activity became qualified opportunities and customers.

You changed the list, rewrote the copy, increased the volume or replaced the tools. The result remained weak, so you reduced the campaign or stopped it.

Now the company says, “We tried outbound. It does not work for us.”

But you still do not know what failed.

The market may have been too small or difficult to reach. The offer may have depended on trust that a cold buyer did not have. The targeting may have been weak. The campaign may not have had accurate contact data or a usable path to the intended buyers. The funnel may have lost good prospects. The customers may have cost too much to acquire.

Changing one component cannot fix an unidentified failure.

**“Outbound failed” is an outcome, not a diagnosis.**

I have run outbound and made mistakes across every part of that system. I chose motions that did not fit the market. I tried to sell offers that did not work cold. I got targeting and signals wrong. I measured the funnel badly. I made mistakes in the economics.

Those mistakes taught me to separate outbound into six conditions. I assembled this framework so you can inspect them individually instead of repeating the same failure with different tools.

This article gives you that diagnostic framework. It will show you:

- what must be true for outbound to work;
- which numbers describe each part of the system;
- what each number measures;
- what assumptions the calculation requires;
- what operating decision the result should change;
- what the number still cannot prove.

The six conditions are:

1. The outbound motion fits the market.
2. The existing offer can sell to a cold buyer.
3. The right buyer has a defensible reason to care.
4. The campaign has accurate contact data and a usable path to that buyer.
5. The complete funnel converts valid contact attempts into qualified opportunities.
6. The customer economics justify the complete acquisition cost.

One weak condition can damage the final result. The purpose of this framework is not to manufacture certainty. It is to replace random changes with a disciplined investigation.

## 1. The outbound motion must fit the market

I made mistakes choosing outbound motions that did not fit the market. That is why the diagnosis begins with the market rather than the volume of messages you can send.

The first question is not how many messages you can send. It is how many suitable companies you can identify, how valuable each customer is and how difficult the buyer is to reach.

Keep three units separate:

- An **account** is a company you may want as a customer.
- A **contact** is a person inside that company.
- A **message** is one attempt to reach one contact.

One account can contain several relevant contacts. One contact can receive several messages. Counting 10,000 email addresses does not prove you have 10,000 suitable accounts.

Start by defining the account rules:

- industries or operating situations that fit;
- company size or commercial capacity that matters;
- geographies you can serve;
- required characteristics;
- hard exclusions;
- buyer roles that can act on the problem;
- at least one credible path to those buyers.

Your reachable market is the number of accounts that pass those rules and have at least one usable path to a relevant buyer. It is smaller than a database result and usually smaller than a broad market estimate.

One distinction I learned from Cole Gordon is that market size, customer value and buyer accessibility should determine the outbound motion.

He contrasts Fortune 500 accounts with real-estate agents advertising on Zillow. The Fortune 500 accounts represent a small, high-value and difficult-to-reach market. The Zillow advertisers represent a larger and more accessible market with lower customer value.

The first can justify low-volume, highly researched and coordinated outreach. The second can support more standardization.

These are operator examples, not thresholds to copy. The principle is what matters.

A small, valuable market may require named-account research, several contacts per account and coordinated email and LinkedIn activity. A larger, accessible market may support more repeatable execution. A mixed market should be divided into tiers instead of forcing every account through the same process.

### Market runway

**Market runway = remaining eligible reachable accounts ÷ newly activated unique accounts per period**

This is an operating definition for this diagnostic, not an industry benchmark.

An account becomes activated when it receives the first planned outbound action. Do not count every contact or follow-up as a new account.

Market runway measures how long the current market can support the planned account volume. If 600 eligible accounts remain and you activate 100 new accounts per month, the simple runway is six months.

The calculation assumes:

- the account rules remain stable;
- the account count is deduplicated;
- activated accounts are tracked correctly;
- previously contacted accounts are not treated as new;
- the period remains consistent.

The number does not predict sales. It tells you whether your activity will exhaust the market before you have enough time to learn.

A short runway changes the operating decision. You may need to lower volume, increase research depth, pursue several relevant people inside each account or use a named-account motion. A longer runway can support more standardization if the offer and economics also work.

The market can be reachable and still be wrong for the proposed motion. The possible customer value must justify the work required to reach it. Section 6 calculates whether it does.

## 2. The existing offer must be able to sell to a cold buyer

I have tried to sell offers that did not work cold. That taught me to separate an offer that works through existing trust from one a stranger can understand, believe and judge.

An offer that sells through referrals or to buyers already familiar with your content or brand has not necessarily proved that it can sell cold.

A referral, familiar brand or history of useful content transfers trust before the sales conversation begins. That trust can answer questions the offer itself never needed to answer. A cold buyer receives none of it.

A cold offer has three parts: what you sell, how you explain it and the terms of the deal. Better copy cannot compensate when the underlying offer is unclear, hard to believe or too risky for a cold buyer.

Start by answering seven questions:

1. **Problem:** Does the buyer recognize a specific problem that matters enough to solve?
2. **Outcome:** What changes after purchasing, and how will the buyer judge the result?
3. **Method:** Can a stranger understand what you will do and why it should work?
4. **Proof:** What relevant evidence supports the actual promise for this buyer?
5. **Risk:** What could the buyer lose in money, time, disruption or reputation?
6. **Time to value:** When will the buyer receive the first useful result, and what must happen first?
7. **First commitment:** How much money, time, access, internal effort or contract length must the buyer commit before experiencing value?

Those answers expose the weaknesses. They are not the finished offer.

### Turn the diagnosis into a cold offer

Use the answers to rebuild the offer in this order:

1. **Choose one specific buyer, one expensive problem and one business result they already want.** Do not ask cold outreach to manufacture desire. Connect an existing problem to a result the buyer already values.

2. **Separate the offer from the channel and delivery format.** Email, LinkedIn, software and consulting describe how you reach the buyer or deliver the work. The offer is the commercial proposal: the result, method, proof, buyer effort, risk, price and terms.

3. **Make the method concrete.** Explain why the buyer's current option fails, what you will do, in what order and why that work should produce a different result. “Our proprietary revenue framework” is a name, not an explanation.

4. **Create a commercial difference.** Give the buyer a real reason to choose the offer through the product, delivery, pricing or risk structure. An invented framework name or cosmetic wording change does not create that difference.

5. **Match every claim to relevant proof.** The evidence should support the same type of buyer, problem, method and result being promised. If it does not, narrow the claim or gather better evidence.

6. **Reduce uncertainty and buyer burden.** Make the scope, responsibilities, time to first value, price, payment terms, exit conditions and any guarantee or performance terms clear. Do not use a guarantee for a result you cannot control.

7. **Match the first commitment to the trust available.** A cold buyer may understand the full service and still reject the money, time, access or operational risk required to start. If the full commitment is too large, consider a bounded first purchase that lets the buyer judge a real result before making the larger decision.

The method, proof and first commitment deserve particular scrutiny.

A recognizable logo may show that somebody trusted you. It does not prove the result you promise. A useful case shows the starting point, work performed, result, conditions and buyer context. A broad claim such as “we help companies scale” proves neither.

A smaller first commitment does not always mean discounting the service. It can be a paid pilot, assessment, implementation or useful component that produces a result on its own.

One bounded example I learned from Daniel Fazio is a short paid trial used as a lower-commitment first yes before a recurring enterprise SaaS outbound engagement. Another structure is trial onboarding, where the buyer experiences the first part of delivery and can leave within a defined period. These are operator examples, not universal requirements.

A smaller first commitment is useful only when:

- it completely solves a real problem;
- the buyer can judge the result;
- the scope and terms are honest;
- it does not hide the larger commercial relationship;
- it naturally connects to the full service.

Before testing the offer, check whether the price can support both customer acquisition and delivery. Also check whether the work can be delivered at higher volume without losing margin or quality. Section 6 shows how to test the acquisition economics.

At the end of this process, you should be able to document the buyer, problem, promised result, method, proof, buyer effort, risk, time to value, first commitment, price and terms.

Mark anything unsupported as unknown.

This gives you a structured cold offer to test. It does not prove demand or predict conversion. A controlled cohort produces the first-party evidence needed to judge how the offer performs.

## 3. The right buyer needs a defensible reason to care

I made targeting and signal mistakes while running outbound. That taught me to separate targeting, signals and personalization because each one answers a different question.

Targeting decides whether the company and person belong in the campaign. A signal can change priority or timing. Personalization communicates the relevant evidence without inventing a fact about the buyer.

Outbound needs three separate decisions:

1. Which companies genuinely fit?
2. Which people can make, influence or sponsor the decision?
3. Why is the problem relevant enough to justify contacting them?

Company headcount, revenue, industry and geography matter when they change the problem, purchasing power, buying process or delivery requirements. They are not reasons for contact by themselves.

The person also needs to fit. A relevant title is a starting point, not proof of authority. Define who owns the problem, who controls the budget, who evaluates the solution and who can block it.

Then establish a truthful reason for contact.

That reason can come from stable fit. A company can belong in the campaign because its business model, operating structure or regulatory position makes the problem consistently relevant. A trigger event is useful, but it is not mandatory.

When a signal is used, it must strengthen the commercial reasoning. Funding, hiring, technology use, marketplace activity or a regulatory change means nothing until it is connected to the problem you solve.

A list-source example makes the distinction clear. A broad list proves only that the people work in real estate. A list of agents advertising on Zillow adds the observable fact that they pay to acquire business through that marketplace.

That still does not prove they need your service. It gives you a more defensible reason to prioritize them than a job title alone.

Inspect every signal through four questions:

- **Connection:** What does this fact establish about the problem, priority or buying behaviour?
- **Reliability:** Can the fact be verified from a legitimate source?
- **Recency:** When was it observed, and is it still relevant?
- **Usability:** Does it change account priority, timing or the reason for contact?

“Congratulations on your funding” is not relevance. The funding becomes relevant only when it produces a change, such as hiring, expansion, compliance work or operational complexity, that connects to the offer.

Every account approved for outreach should answer:

- Why does this company fit?
- Which person should care?
- What decision can that person make?
- What problem are we connecting to?
- What evidence supports the reason for contact?
- When was the evidence observed?
- What would disqualify the account?
- Which facts remain unknown?

Do not turn assumptions into personalization. If the evidence does not establish that the company has a problem, describe the observable situation instead of asserting the problem as fact.

## 4. The campaign needs a usable path to the buyer

You can choose the right market, build an offer a cold buyer can judge and identify the right company and person, then still learn little from the campaign.

The list looks ready. Emails are marked delivered. LinkedIn requests are sent. Replies remain weak, so the message gets blamed.

But those events do not prove the same thing. A contact record shows that somebody was found. A sent event shows that an attempt was made. Neither proves that the intended buyer received or read the outreach.

If the campaign could not make a valid contact attempt to the intended buyer, changing the offer or message is premature.

Cold email and LinkedIn are the channels I can speak from directly. I learned to separate four facts: a contact was found, a usable path was verified, a technical event was recorded and a human responded.

### Verify buyer-path coverage

**Verified buyer-path coverage = qualified accounts with at least one verified contact path to a relevant buyer ÷ qualified accounts researched**

This is an operating definition for this diagnostic, not an industry benchmark.

For email, a verified path means a business address tied to an approved buyer role and checked according to the campaign's verification standard. For LinkedIn, it means the intended person has been identified and the campaign has an available route through an existing connection, connection request or InMail. One account may have both paths, one or neither.

The calculation tells you how much of the qualified account list can enter the planned campaign. It assumes that account fit and buyer roles were approved first, accounts were deduplicated and contact paths were verified consistently.

Use it to compare list sources, expose missing contact data or decide whether an account needs another relevant contact. It does not prove delivery, attention, response or interest.

### Calculate the cost of producing contactable accounts

**Cost per contactable account = attributable research, data and verification cost ÷ qualified accounts with at least one verified buyer path**

This is also an operating definition. It measures what you spend to produce one qualified account that can enter the planned campaign.

Allocate shared research, software and setup costs consistently. Use the result to compare data sources or research methods. Do not use it to predict sales or declare that one channel works.

A cheap database record and a verified route to the intended buyer are different outputs.

### Keep technical delivery separate from human reach

For email, Google requires SPF or DKIM for all senders to personal Gmail accounts. It requires SPF, DKIM and DMARC for senders above its bulk-sender threshold. Microsoft states that authentication does not guarantee delivery. AWS defines a delivery event as delivery to the recipient mail server.

Therefore, a delivered email does not prove primary-inbox placement, reading or buyer attention. Authentication is a sending requirement, not evidence that the intended buyer saw the message.

For LinkedIn, finding a profile, sending a connection request, gaining an accepted connection, sending a message and receiving a response are separate events.

This section establishes whether the campaign could make a valid contact attempt. The next section follows those attempts through responses, conversations, meetings, opportunities and customers.

## 5. The complete funnel must convert contact attempts into qualified opportunities

I made funnel-measurement mistakes while running outbound. That is why this framework keeps each channel’s early events separate and then follows one cohort through to customers.

These are operating definitions for comparing your own cohorts. They are not universal industry benchmarks.

A response rate, meeting count or close rate cannot describe the complete result.

Track one cohort from its original entry until it has had enough time to produce customers. A cohort should share the same segment, channel, offer, list source, operating conditions and attribution window.

The original cohort entry must be fixed before outreach:

- **Cold email:** unique prospects emailed.
- **LinkedIn:** unique prospects for whom the first planned LinkedIn action was recorded.
- **Named-account outreach:** activated accounts.

Do not begin the cohort with a later contact event. That would remove earlier contact-data and path failures from the final customer rate.

The complete chain is:

Original cohort entry\
→ channel-specific contact event\
→ relevant response\
→ qualified conversation\
→ meeting booked\
→ meeting held\
→ qualified opportunity\
→ customer

Keep the channel-specific early stages separate. Once a qualified conversation begins, the channels can share the same downstream stages.

### Cold email

#### Server-delivery rate

**Formula:** unique prospects with at least one email delivered to the recipient mail server ÷ unique prospects emailed.

**Meaning:** the share of emailed prospects for whom the sending system recorded delivery to the recipient mail server. It does not prove inbox placement, reading or buyer attention.

**Assumptions:** prospects are deduplicated, several emails to one prospect count once, delivery events are recorded correctly and the observation window is fixed.

**What to investigate:** contact accuracy, bounces, authentication, sending configuration and sender reputation. A weak rate identifies a technical delivery problem, but it cannot distinguish between those causes by itself.

#### Positive-reply rate

**Formula:** unique prospects with a relevant positive reply ÷ unique prospects with at least one email delivered to the recipient mail server.

**Meaning:** the share of technically delivered prospects who expressed relevant interest.

**Assumptions:** “positive” is defined before replies are classified, automatic responses are excluded and each prospect is counted once.

**What to investigate:** company fit, buyer fit, timing, reason for contact, offer, proof and message. The rate cannot identify which one caused the result.

#### Cold-email qualified-conversation rate

**Formula:** unique prospects entering a qualified conversation ÷ unique prospects with at least one email delivered to the recipient mail server.

**Meaning:** how often technically delivered email creates a commercially relevant conversation rather than any reply.

**Assumptions:** qualification criteria are defined before classification, prospects are deduplicated and the same criteria are used across the cohort.

**What to investigate:** targeting, qualification rules and whether positive replies are becoming real sales conversations. Review the underlying replies before deciding what failed.

### LinkedIn

A connection request, accepted connection, InMail and direct message are different events. Choose the path and write the exact contact event into the cohort definition before launch, such as a connection request sent, a message sent after connection acceptance or an InMail sent. None of these events proves attention.

#### LinkedIn relevant-response rate

**Formula:** prospects providing a relevant response ÷ prospects meeting the predefined LinkedIn contact event.

**Meaning:** whether prospects meeting the chosen contact event engage with the commercial reason for contact.

**Assumptions:** the exact contact event remains fixed, prospects are deduplicated and relevant responses are defined before classification.

**What to investigate:** profile credibility, company fit, buyer fit, timing, reason for contact and message. The rate cannot distinguish between them.

#### LinkedIn qualified-conversation rate

**Formula:** qualified conversations ÷ prospects meeting the predefined LinkedIn contact event.

**Meaning:** how often LinkedIn creates a commercially relevant conversation rather than a casual or irrelevant exchange.

**Assumptions:** the exact contact event and qualification rules are fixed before launch, each prospect is counted once and the same outreach path is used throughout the cohort.

**What to investigate:** buyer fit, qualification and whether relevant responses are becoming commercial discussions. The number cannot explain the failure without reviewing the conversations.

### Named-account outreach across channels

#### Named-account contact-attempt rate

**Account contact-attempt rate = activated accounts where at least one valid contact attempt to an intended buyer was recorded ÷ accounts activated**

An activated account has received the first action required by the account plan. Recording valid contact attempts for three people inside one company still counts as one account.

This tells you whether the plan is producing valid contact attempts inside the intended companies. It assumes that account activation, intended buyer roles and the exact contact event were defined before launch.

A weak result should make you inspect contact coverage, buyer roles, the available contact paths and execution. It does not prove human attention, buyer authority or account interest.

#### Named-account relevant engagement

**Relevant-engagement rate = accounts with a relevant two-way engagement ÷ accounts activated**

This measures how often activated accounts produce meaningful engagement.

Relevant engagement must be defined before launch. Several replies from one company still count as one engaged account.

A weak result should make you inspect account selection, buyer access, the reason for contact and the coordinated outreach plan. It does not identify which one caused the result.

### Common downstream stages

#### Conversation-to-booking rate

**Formula:** meetings booked ÷ qualified conversations.

**Meaning:** whether a commercially relevant conversation becomes a scheduled meeting.

**Assumptions:** conversations and bookings belong to the same cohort, bookings are deduplicated and the observation window allows enough time for booking.

**What to investigate:** the request, response handling, qualification path, first commitment and scheduling friction. The number cannot tell you whether the prospect would ultimately buy.

#### Attendance rate

**Formula:** meetings held ÷ meetings booked.

**Meaning:** whether scheduled meetings happen.

**Assumptions:** bookings and held meetings use the same observation window, rescheduled meetings are handled consistently and duplicates are removed.

**What to investigate:** qualification depth, meeting purpose, scheduling, expectations and follow-up. The result does not prove that reminders alone will solve the problem.

#### Opportunity rate

**Formula:** qualified opportunities ÷ meetings held.

**Meaning:** whether held meetings contain a real buyer, relevant problem and possible transaction.

A qualified opportunity must be defined before measurement. The definition should include the buyer, problem, authority, commercial fit and an agreed next step.

**Assumptions:** opportunity criteria are fixed, meetings and opportunities belong to the same cohort and opportunities are not counted twice.

**What to investigate:** buyer authority, problem relevance, account selection, qualification and discovery. The rate cannot prove that either the lead source or sales process is solely responsible.

#### Win rate

**Formula:** new customers ÷ qualified opportunities.

**Meaning:** whether qualified opportunities become customers.

**Assumptions:** the cohort has matured under the company’s normal sales cycle, a win has one fixed definition and customers use the same attribution window.

**What to investigate:** proof, risk, terms, the decision process, sales execution and the offer. The rate cannot distinguish between an offer problem and a sales-execution problem by itself.

#### Customer rate

**Formula:** new customers ÷ original cohort entries.

The original cohort entry is:

- unique prospects emailed for cold email;
- unique prospects for whom the first planned LinkedIn action was recorded;
- activated accounts for named-account outreach.

**Meaning:** the complete customer yield from the original cohort, including every failure before the contact event, response, qualification and conversion.

**Assumptions:** the original-entry definition was fixed before launch, the cohort has matured, customers are attributed to the same cohort and the denominator does not change between campaigns.

**What to investigate:** use the earlier stage rates to locate where customers are being lost. Customer rate alone cannot identify the cause.

If a denominator is zero, the rate is not applicable. If a stage was not tracked, the result is unknown. Neither should be entered as zero.

Cole Gordon uses an inbound appointment-handoff example to show why the complete chain matters.

In the direct path, 100 bookings multiplied by 70% attendance and 25% closing produce 17.5 customers. In a path with another qualification handoff, 100 bookings multiplied by 70% attendance, 78% rebooking, another 70% attendance and 40% closing produce about 15.3 customers.

Although the final close rate is higher in the second path, it produces fewer customers because the additional handoff creates more leakage.

This example concerns an inbound booked-meeting funnel, not a cold-outbound benchmark. Its lesson is mathematical: improving one downstream rate can still damage the complete result.

The earliest weak stage tells you where to investigate first. It does not prove the cause.

## 6. The customer economics must justify the complete acquisition cost

I made economics mistakes while running outbound. That is why this framework keeps the acquisition cost and customer value inside the same cohort and leaves missing inputs unknown.

These economic calculations apply to cold email, LinkedIn and named-account outreach. Only the original cohort-entry unit changes by motion.

A campaign is not commercially sound because it produced replies, meetings or even customers. The cost and value must come from the same cohort.

A matured cohort has had enough time for its qualified opportunities to reach a win or loss under the company’s normal sales cycle.

Include every acquisition cost attributable to that cohort:

- account and contact data;
- research and verification;
- channel infrastructure;
- software;
- internal labour;
- contractors or agency fees;
- management and review time;
- allocated setup costs.

Do not count software and data while treating human work as free. Apply the same allocation method when comparing cohorts.

The calculations below are transparent definitions within this diagnostic framework. They do not depend on borrowed benchmarks.

### Customer acquisition cost

**CAC = complete cohort-attributable acquisition cost ÷ customers acquired from the same matured cohort**

CAC measures what one acquired customer cost through that cohort.

Costs and customers must use the same attribution window. Shared costs must be allocated consistently.

If the cohort has produced zero customers, CAC is unknown or undefined. The campaign cost is real, but CAC is not zero.

CAC changes the economic decision. It shows whether the current acquisition method costs more than the customer can support.

It does not show how valuable the customer is or how quickly the acquisition cost returns.

### First-year gross profit per customer

**Expected first-year gross profit per customer = expected collected first-year revenue minus direct first-year delivery costs**

Use collected or realistically collectible revenue, not the largest contract value quoted in the pipeline. Direct delivery costs should include costs that increase because the customer was acquired.

If gross margin is known and applies consistently, you may use:

**Expected first-year gross profit = expected collected first-year revenue × gross margin**

This is a shortcut. It does not replace an understanding of the underlying delivery costs.

First-year gross profit places an upper boundary on what the customer can support in acquisition cost during the first year before overhead and the required profit are considered.

For historical cohorts, use actual collections and delivery costs. For projections, label the pricing, collection and delivery assumptions.

### Expected gross profit per held meeting

**Expected first-year gross profit per held meeting = expected first-year gross profit per customer × customers acquired ÷ meetings held**

All figures must come from the same matured cohort.

When the customer and meeting counts are actual but customer gross profit is expected, label the result as mixed rather than fully historical.

This measures the average first-year gross profit associated with each held meeting.

It does not tell you what you should pay for a meeting. Remaining sales costs, acquisition costs and the required profit buffer still need to be deducted.

Use it to judge whether meeting production is creating enough economic value to support its cost.

### Gross-profit LTV:CAC

**Gross-profit LTV:CAC = lifetime gross profit per customer ÷ CAC**

Revenue LTV:CAC will look stronger because it ignores delivery cost. Label it clearly if you calculate it, but use gross-profit LTV:CAC for the economic decision.

Lifetime gross profit requires a defined retention period, expected collections and direct delivery costs. A projected lifetime value must label those assumptions.

This metric tells you whether long-term customer value can support the acquisition cost.

It does not show how quickly the cash returns. There is no universal ratio that decides whether outbound works. The acceptable relationship depends on available cash, retention reliability, sales risk and payback time.

### CAC payback

CAC payback is the first point at which cumulative realized or expected gross profit per acquired customer recovers CAC.

For historical cohorts, use realized gross profit by month. For projections, label the collection, delivery-cost and timing assumptions.

When monthly gross profit is expected to remain stable, a simplified calculation is:

**Simplified payback months = CAC ÷ expected monthly gross profit per customer**

The stability assumption matters. This shortcut becomes misleading when onboarding fees, annual prepayments, seasonal delivery or early churn change the monthly pattern.

Gross-profit payback measures how long the company finances acquisition before recovering the cost through gross profit. It is not automatically cash payback unless the calculation uses actual cash collections and cash costs.

### Break-even customers

First define contribution.

For this calculation, contribution per customer means the gross profit expected within a stated period after subtracting the additional post-acquisition sales or onboarding costs included in the decision.

**Break-even customers = ceiling(campaign cost ÷ defined contribution per customer)**

The time horizon must be explicit. A first-year calculation cannot silently use lifetime contribution.

This measures the minimum whole customers required for the campaign to recover its defined cost.

It does not estimate the likelihood of acquiring them. If contribution is zero or negative, the campaign cannot break even under the entered assumptions.

### Cost per cohort entry

**Cost per cohort entry = total pilot cost ÷ original cohort entries**

The original cohort entry is:

- unique prospects emailed for cold email;
- unique prospects for whom the first planned LinkedIn action was recorded;
- activated accounts for named-account outreach.

This measures how much the pilot spends for each original entry before any contact event, response or conversion failure occurs.

The calculation assumes that all attributable pilot costs are included, entries are deduplicated and the cost and entry count use the same cohort and period.

Use it to connect pilot cost with the customer rate the economics require.

It does not measure the quality of each entry. A lower cost per entry can still produce worse economics if the entries convert poorly.

### Required customer rate

When no previous outbound cohort exists, do not invent conversion rates. Calculate what the pilot would need to achieve.

**Required customer rate = cost per cohort entry ÷ expected contribution per customer**

The cost per cohort entry must use the channel-specific original entry defined above. Expected contribution per customer must use the same time horizon as the pilot decision.

This measures the minimum share of original cohort entries that must become customers for the pilot to recover its cost within the defined period.

It does not predict success. If the required customer rate exceeds 100%, the economics are impossible under the entered assumptions. If the rate is lower, it remains a hypothesis for the first controlled cohort to test.

A simple economic rule is to compare the additional value of the next unit of activity with its additional cost.

Increase volume only while the added activity creates enough additional value. When additional cost rises faster than additional value, improve the system rather than buying more activity.

## Use the failure point to decide what to investigate

The observations below do not provide automatic diagnoses. They identify the next questions worth inspecting.

**The reachable-market runway is too short.**\
Inspect the account rules, exclusions, account tiers, research depth, additional defensible segments and planned activation volume. The runway cannot tell you whether any account will buy.

**Few accounts have a verified path to the buyer.**\
Inspect the buyer roles, data sources, verification rules and available contact paths. The number cannot tell you whether the offer is relevant or whether the buyer saw the outreach.

**Cold-email server delivery is weak.**\
Inspect contact accuracy, bounces, authentication, sending configuration and sender reputation. The result cannot distinguish between them or prove inbox placement.

**Too few LinkedIn prospects or named accounts complete the predefined contact event.**\
Inspect the predefined outreach path, profile credibility, contact coverage, buyer roles and execution. The result cannot tell you whether the offer is relevant or whether the buyer paid attention.

**Valid contact attempts are recorded but relevant responses are weak.**\
Inspect company fit, person fit, the reason for contact, timing, offer, proof and message. Do not assume the copy is solely responsible.

**Qualified conversations rarely become meetings.**\
Inspect the request, response handling, qualification path, first commitment and scheduling friction. The number cannot tell you whether the prospect would ultimately buy.

**Booked meetings do not hold.**\
Inspect qualification depth, meeting purpose, scheduling, reminders and expectations. The result does not prove that reminders alone will solve it.

**Held meetings rarely become qualified opportunities.**\
Inspect buyer authority, problem relevance, qualification rules and discovery. The number cannot prove that either the lead source or sales process is solely responsible.

**Qualified opportunities rarely become customers.**\
Inspect the offer, proof, risk, terms, decision process and sales execution. The win rate cannot distinguish between an offer problem and a sales-execution problem by itself.

**Customers are acquired at an uneconomic cost.**\
Inspect fully loaded CAC, customer contribution, retention, labour, tooling and process complexity. Do not assume that adding volume will improve the economics.

Review the underlying accounts, responses, meetings and sales outcomes before changing the system. Then change one major condition in the next controlled cohort while keeping the remaining definitions stable.

AI must not invent benchmarks or autonomously choose the offer, price, channel or contract. It can organize evidence and perform defined work. It cannot replace the commercial judgment required when several explanations still fit the evidence.

## The outbound failure review, in order

Before you replace another tool, rewrite the campaign or abandon outbound, answer these six questions:

1. Does the outbound motion match the reachable market, customer value and buyer accessibility?
2. Can the existing offer earn attention and trust from a buyer with no prior relationship?
3. Does every approved prospect have verified fit and a defensible reason for contact?
4. Did the campaign have accurate contact data and a usable path to the intended buyer?
5. Where does the complete cohort lose buyers before they become customers?
6. Can the resulting customers repay the complete acquisition cost within an acceptable period?

I assembled this framework after making mistakes across those conditions and learning how to inspect them separately.

Do not use this guide as a reason to delay or abandon outbound.

Outbound can become a reliable, repeatable acquisition channel when the market, offer, targeting, buyer access, funnel and economics support it. You do not need every formula on day one. Start with one reachable segment, one buyer, one offer, one channel and one cohort tracked from first contact to customer. Add the deeper calculations as volume and complexity grow.

The point is not to make outbound feel heavier. It is to stop another campaign from failing without telling you why.
