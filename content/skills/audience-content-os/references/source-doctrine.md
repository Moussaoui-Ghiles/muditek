# Audience Content Source Doctrine

## Source Boundary

Primary source: *$100M Leads: How to Get Strangers to Want to Buy Your Stuff*, “Post Free Content Part I — How To Build An Audience To Get Engaged Leads” and “Post Free Content Part II — Monetize Your Audience,” including the “Free Goodwill” interlude.

- PDF pages: 109–157
- Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads* (2023), pages 109-157.

This reference paraphrases the source's operating logic. It is not a general content-marketing synthesis. Do not add outside tactics and attribute them to the source.

Maintain four evidence labels:

- **Doctrine:** the source directly prescribes or defines it.
- **Example:** the source demonstrates the idea; it is not automatically a rule.
- **Source assertion:** the source states a causal or general claim without independent proof in these chapters.
- **Self-reported result or preference:** the source describes the author's own outcome, experience, or favored method.

## Governing Mental Models

### Learn from the people already producing the result

The source opens by contrasting rationalizations with learning from people who outperform you. The operating advice is to study the behavior of people achieving the result and use paid knowledge or experience to save time. Treat this as a source philosophy, not proof that every successful creator's method transfers unchanged.

### The audience compounds, not the individual post

An individual piece of content ages. The accumulated audience is the asset that can compound. Regular publishing grows the number of people who know you; those people can share the work, creating more warm awareness and more engaged leads.

### Free content is one-to-many warm outreach

Warm outreach communicates individually with people who already know you. Free content communicates with many such people simultaneously. The basic loop is:

```text
publish useful content
  -> people consume and share
  -> warm audience grows
  -> occasional matched offer
  -> engaged leads
  -> revenue
```

The chapters also claim that relevant content makes other advertising more effective because a prospect who investigates and finds useful material becomes more likely to buy. Treat that causal claim as a source assertion unless first-party evidence proves it in the current business.

### More volume creates more chances to learn and grow

The source's central practical advice is to publish regularly on every relevant platform and to increase output. It presents no hidden mechanism beyond doing more useful content more often.

The source reports that an initial twelve months added roughly 200,000 audience members, while a later increase to about ten times the content output added roughly 1.2 million over six months and accelerated growth about tenfold. This is a self-reported case, not a forecast or required result.

### Know the trade-offs

Free content reaches more people but is less personalized than one-to-one outreach, so a smaller percentage may respond. It also competes with all other free material. Successful ideas are copied, requiring continued innovation.

## The Content Unit

All audience-growing content rewards the consumer. Before reward can occur, the consumer needs a reason to begin and must remain long enough to receive it. Reverse that sequence into three components:

1. **Hook:** redirect attention to the content.
2. **Retain:** keep attention through consumption.
3. **Reward:** satisfy the reason the person consumed it.

A content unit is the smallest piece of material that accomplishes all three. It can be an image, meme, sentence, jingle, short clip, or another tiny unit. Hook, retention, and reward can occur almost simultaneously.

Long content is a sequence of linked content units. It is not exempt from Hook–Retain–Reward; it must perform the cycle repeatedly.

## Hook

### Objective and measure

The hook gives someone a reason to redirect attention from every alternative available to them. Its basic measurement is the proportion of exposed people who begin consuming.

Improve the hook through three levers:

1. topic;
2. headline or opening promise;
3. format.

### Topic: prefer personal experience

The easiest defensible differentiation is material nobody else can claim because nobody else has lived the user's life. The source divides topics into five sources.

#### Far Past

Use important lessons from earlier life. Connect the wisdom to the product, service, or audience problem so people receive the lesson without having to experience the same damage.

The source's phone-usage story demonstrates a complete miniature unit: a complaint about having no time creates the hook; a friend checking usage retains attention; the discovery of hours spent on social media rewards it. The story works because the struggle is recognizable and the lesson serves the audience.

Do not copy the story. Find an actual past event belonging to the user.

#### Recent Past

Do work, then explain what was done or what happened. Review the previous week, including calendar events, meetings, conversations, social interactions, and warm outreach. Preserve notes, recordings, and records so the content can be specific.

Testimonials and case studies fit here when the story itself provides value and can truthfully connect to the service. A promotional outcome alone is not enough; the audience still needs a reward.

#### Present

Capture an idea when it occurs. Keep a dependable capture method close enough to use immediately, even if that means briefly pausing a meeting. Publish the idea and observe response. If a post materially outperforms the user's normal work, treat the topic as evidence of interest and create more around it.

#### Trending

Go where attention already exists, then contribute experience or expertise that is relevant to the trend. The purpose is access to broader attention, not unrelated commentary. Do not force an opinion merely because a topic is popular.

#### Manufactured

Choose a topic people find interesting, then learn it, make it, or do it and show the work. This demands the most time and effort and may produce the largest reward.

The source's visual note says manufactured content has the greatest growth and monetization potential because skilled creators can engineer the reward. It points to music videos dominating a list of highly viewed videos as an example of highly manufactured material. This is an example and source conclusion, not a promise.

For most entrepreneurs, the source prefers documenting real life because it costs less, supports greater volume, and is more sustainable over a lifetime. Its action is to use life as the raw material and share it.

### Headline: communicate the expected reward

The headline explains why consuming this piece is a better use of attention than the alternatives. The source avoids fill-in-the-blank templates and instead borrows seven durable news components:

- **Recency:** something new or newly relevant.
- **Relevancy:** direct importance to the intended person.
- **Prominence:** a recognizable person, organization, or authority.
- **Proximity:** closeness to the audience's place, identity, or situation.
- **Conflict:** opposed forces, opinions, incentives, or outcomes.
- **Unusualness:** something surprising or outside expectation.
- **Ongoing development:** an unresolved, changing story with possible turns.

The chapter's main text suggests trying to combine at least two. Its action step says to include one or more. Preserve that nuance: one is the stated minimum in the action, while two or more is the stronger experimentation suggestion. Do not invent a stricter rule.

### Format: resemble content people expect to reward them

Start with the platform, then the intended audience. Study the best-performing platform-native material aimed at that market and use its familiar form as the guide. People are more willing to begin content whose format resembles other content that has rewarded them.

The source states that using topic, headline, and format well placed the author in a perceived top one percent. That is a self-assessment, not an objective benchmark.

## Retain

### Curiosity is the primary mechanism

Curiosity comes from an unresolved question, explicit or implicit. Retention keeps the audience moving toward its resolution. The source uses three primary structures.

### Lists

A list presents related things, facts, tips, opinions, ideas, or other items around a theme. Reveal the number in the headline or opening seconds. The remaining count gives the audience an open question: what are all the items?

The source reports from experience that numbered material retained people longer. Treat this as a source observation.

### Steps

Steps are ordered actions that accomplish a result. If early steps are clear and valuable, the consumer wants the remaining steps needed for the outcome.

Steps differ from lists:

- steps require a sequence and promise a more explicit result, but are less flexible;
- lists can contain many kinds of items in flexible order, but often have a less explicit result.

Do not label an unordered collection as steps.

### Stories

Stories describe events, real or imagined. They may concern what happened, what could happen, or what could never happen. Stories worth telling usually contain a lesson or takeaway. Their natural retention question is what happens next.

For first-person business content, use only real, supported stories. Imaginary stories may illustrate an idea only when clearly framed as hypothetical.

### Combine the structures

Lists, steps, and stories can interweave:

- a list can sit inside a step;
- a story can make each item concrete;
- a story can demonstrate or reinforce a step;
- a piece can present a list of stories;
- a long piece can carry several storylines.

Use combinations to clarify and increase reward, not to add ornamental complexity.

## Reward

### The three reward questions

Ask:

1. Is the material genuinely good for this audience?
2. Does it satisfy the reason the person started?
3. Would the person have a reason to share it?

The audience, not the creator, determines the answer through behavior.

### Value per unit of attention

Quality increases with the frequency of useful reward during the time consumed. The source calls this value per second. The practical consequence is not that everything must be short. There is no content that is inherently too long; content becomes too boring when rewards are too sparse or weak.

### Match or exceed the promise

Increase the chance of reward by:

- choosing the correct audience and topic;
- using an accurate headline and expected format;
- retaining with a list, steps, a story, or a combination;
- completely resolving the question created by the hook.

Reward fails when the delivery does not match the promise. The source illustrates failures such as promising seven items but giving four, providing seven weak or familiar items, addressing people who cannot use the advice, or prescribing tactics inaccessible to the intended professional.

Provide more usable value than the alternatives. Make good on every promise and answer every meaningful unresolved question.

### Let growth judge the work

The source treats audience growth as the practical quality test: if the audience does not grow, the content is not good enough yet. Practice, learn from response, and improve.

Do not extend that conclusion into commercial validation. Audience growth can evaluate attention and reward; it cannot by itself prove the right buyer, a validated problem, or revenue.

### Short and long content

Short material contains fewer content units. Long material links more units and therefore demands more skill. Begin by mastering small units even if the intended medium is long. The source draws an analogy to writers of large works first learning smaller forms.

Once the unit is understood, create more units and link them without allowing reward density to collapse.

### Write for strangers

Assume every person has never heard of the creator. Strangers can understand and share content made for them. Insider jokes, unexplained references, and dependence on previous posts make outsiders feel excluded and slow audience growth. Existing followers generally tolerate useful reminders.

## Monetization: Give and Ask

### Goodwill as deposits and withdrawals

Useful content deposits goodwill. Commercial interruptions withdraw it. The source compares established media with approximate ratios: television offers around 47 minutes of programming to 13 minutes of advertising, and a social feed may show about four posts for one advertisement. These examples describe monetization floors of mature platforms, not an ideal growth target.

Growing platforms tend to over-give and under-ask. The source's preferred strategy is to give until people ask.

### Why the source prefers giving until asked

Giving without an immediate request builds trust and sharing. Reciprocity may prompt people to visit the site, send a message, or email without being pushed. The source says the best customers are often the biggest givers because they feel they received the better end of the exchange.

The claim that this approach prevents growth from slowing is a source assertion, not a guaranteed outcome.

The concise operating principle is: give publicly and let interested people ask privately. Patience preserves audience growth and can improve the eventual response to an offer.

### Ask when the business needs revenue

The source acknowledges that people may need to pay rent or support a family. Asking is allowed. An ask is financially free but costs goodwill, audience growth, and the time that could have built more audience. Make that trade consciously.

## Two Ways to Ask

### Integrated asks

An integrated ask appears inside a valuable piece. A high give-to-ask ratio can support an ask in every piece. The source uses a long podcast with three short advertisements as an example: roughly 58.5 minutes of value to 1.5 minutes of asking, far above a three-to-one ratio.

Another podcast is described as shrinking after asking too much. The lesson is to protect goodwill through over-giving, not to assume one universal numeric threshold.

Place an integrated ask after a valuable moment or at the end. Begin with one ask and watch audience growth before considering a second. The source favors a postscript because it is frequently read; this is a preference, not a requirement.

### Intermittent asks

Publish multiple pure gives, then devote a separate piece to promotion. The source offers ten gives followed by an eleventh promotional post as an example. Short-form platforms often favor intermittent asking, while longer formats can integrate interruptions more naturally.

Do not convert the example into a mandatory ten-to-one rule.

### What to promote

Promote only the core offer or a matched lead magnet.

A lead-magnet ask should extend the value of the current piece. The source example gives one lead tip, then offers eleven more in a polished visual. After opt-in, a thank-you page can present the paid offer in a video. Relevance to the content improves the fit.

A direct offer example contains:

- a clearly identified group of intended buyers;
- a specific desired outcome;
- reduced time delay;
- reduced effort or sacrifice;
- a real guarantee or risk reversal;
- a reason the seller can stand behind that guarantee;
- one response path, such as a direct message, call, comment, or email.

These are example components, not permission to invent scarcity, outcomes, or guarantees. Use only facts the current offer can support.

After an integrated ask, return to value if the content continues.

When unsure between a core offer and lead magnet, the source presents the lead magnet as the lower-risk ask.

### One call to action

Ask for one action at a time. Do not simultaneously request a like, share, subscription, comment, and purchase. If sharing is the action, ask only for the share. If buying is the action, ask only for the purchase. Decide so the audience does not have to choose among competing actions.

## Scaling Distribution

### Depth then width

1. Choose one relevant platform.
2. publish regularly;
3. maximize quality and quantity there;
4. add another relevant platform while maintaining the first;
5. repeat.

The source gives volume examples of up to ten short pieces per day per platform and long material up to five days per week. They illustrate what maximizing may look like; they are not universal requirements.

Advantages:

- concentrates resources;
- maximizes return from one platform;
- allows learning and audience compounding before adding complexity.

Costs:

- delays low-hanging attention on new platforms;
- reduces omnipresence;
- creates single-channel risk if rules change or an account is restricted.

### Width then depth

1. Choose a relevant platform and publish regularly.
2. Move to the next relevant platform before maximizing the first, while maintaining it.
3. Repeat until all relevant platforms are active.
4. Then maximize quality and quantity across them.

Advantages:

- builds broader awareness faster;
- allows light repurposing when native formats are similar, such as one short video adapted across short-video platforms.

Costs:

- consumes more labor, attention, and time;
- risks publishing mediocre material everywhere.

Larger businesses can scale faster because they can assign more resources. Regardless of path, the source says to begin now rather than wait and to increase through the steps over time.

### Batch while keeping the system simple

The source's author note describes a personal batch:

- roughly one hour finding topics through the five sources;
- twice monthly, recording about thirty short clips;
- on the same day, recording two to four longer videos that expand written ideas with stories and examples.

This is a self-reported operating example. The transferable instruction is to start simply and add volume, not to copy the exact calendar.

## Why Content Matters Beyond Direct Leads

The source reports that stopping gym content weakened paid advertising. A first-party survey reportedly found that 78 percent of clients had consumed at least two long-form pieces before booking. These are self-reported observations, not universal attribution proof.

The operating claim is that relevant content nurtures demand:

- strangers find, value, and share it;
- people considering a purchase become warmer;
- people first reached through cold methods can investigate further;
- all advertising may perform better when relevant content exists.

Content must remain relevant to the offer and audience for this supporting effect to be plausible.

## Seven Additional Lessons

### 1. Prefer “how I” before “how to”

Especially when starting, describe what the user has done or prefers instead of declaring what strangers must do. “How I” and “my favorite” are grounded in experience, harder to dispute, and less preachy than universal claims.

Use this only when the experience is real. Do not put the user in first person merely to imitate the form.

### 2. Remind more than you teach

Not everyone sees or remembers every piece. The source reports posting daily about a book while roughly one in five audience members still did not know it existed. Creators become bored with a message before the audience has absorbed it. Repeat useful truths and assume new strangers arrive continuously.

### 3. Begin narrow, then expand

The source uses a scale of puddles, ponds, lakes, and oceans. Win relevance in a smaller niche or local audience before broadening. Expansion can happen over time; broadness is not the starting requirement.

### 4. Turn winning content into sales tools

Identify the greatest hits and label each by the problem it resolves or benefit it creates. Salespeople can send the right piece before or after calls, especially when it resolves a recurring concern. Content therefore serves both audience growth and sales enablement.

### 5. Free content affects paying-customer retention

Customers may consume free content after buying. If it creates value, it can increase perceived return; if it disappoints, it can reduce loyalty. Free material is part of the customer experience, not only acquisition.

### 6. Standards rose; attention spans did not simply disappear

The source rejects the simplistic claim that people cannot pay attention. They will stay with material that continues to reward them. Again: there is no inherently excessive length, only insufficient reward for the time asked.

### 7. Test manual publishing instead of prescheduling

The source reports that manually published posts performed better and theorizes that immediate feedback pressure improved quality. It explicitly invites the reader to try it. Treat this as a self-reported test and hypothesis, not a platform fact.

## Measurement

### Track size and speed monthly

Measure:

- total followers or total reach: how large the audience is;
- the rate of follower or reach growth: how fast it is growing.

Track both absolute and relative change. Moving from 1,000 to 1,500 is an increase of 500 and 50 percent. Moving from 10 to 15 is only five people but is also 50 percent.

### Control inputs

Output comparisons are useful only when publishing inputs are reasonably consistent. Choose a posting cadence and ask cadence per platform, begin, and do not stop prematurely.

The source reports publishing a podcast twice weekly for four years before it reached a top-100 position, then reaching a top-10 United States business position and top-500 global position in year five. This is a persistence example and self-reported ranking, not a promised timeline.

Measure widely, adapt to feedback, and give the system time.

## First Content and Immediate Operating Sequence

The source says the reader's first post can contain an ask because the book has already delivered value. Outside that context, if the creator has not yet earned goodwill or an engaged lead, give value first and ask later.

The source warns that its first-post example is not ideal and should not be copied. Do not fear irrelevant opinions; favor the people helped by the work and the courage required to publish.

Content is less predictable than individual warm outreach but complements it. Continue both:

1. conduct warm outreach to everyone the creator is permitted to contact;
2. publish successes, lessons, and truthful testimonials from early clients;
3. provide useful value;
4. ask occasionally;
5. repeat both daily.

The source claims warm outreach and free content can eventually build a six- or seven-figure business, with cold outreach available for speed. Treat the revenue claim as a source assertion, not a forecast.

## The Source's Daily Visual

The chapter summarizes free-content activity as:

| Question | Source answer |
|---|---|
| Who | Yourself |
| What | Value; give until they ask |
| Where | Any media platform |
| To whom | People who already follow you |
| When | Every morning, seven days per week |
| Why | Build goodwill and get engaged leads |
| How | Written, image, video, or audio posts |
| How much | 100 minutes per day |
| How many | As many times as the platform will show it |
| How long | As long as it takes |

This is the source's summary prescription. Reconcile it with the project's channel cadence rather than silently replacing an existing operating decision.

## Demonstrated Goodwill Asks

The chapters demonstrate rather than merely describe asks.

### Integrated lead-magnet ask

A boxed free-content-training offer appears within the chapter and uses a direct link or QR path. It is a live example of an integrated lead-magnet interruption.

### Review ask

The “Free Goodwill” interlude:

- states that giving without immediate expectation produces longer, happier, and more profitable relationships; treat this as a source assertion;
- opens an unresolved question about who benefits from the next gift;
- identifies the beneficiary as another reader like the current reader once was;
- connects the request to a mission;
- asks for one review, not money, and frames the action as taking less than a minute;
- explains the perceived consequence of the action;
- gives channel-specific instructions for Audible, Kindle, Amazon, and a QR path;
- welcomes the participant into an identity or group;
- expresses gratitude;
- returns to the educational content;
- adds a postscript asking the reader to share the book.

The interlude is evidence of how the source constructs an ask. It does not establish that every ask must use all of these elements. It also contains two actions across the main request and postscript, which should not be used to erase the chapter's explicit one-CTA guidance for a single content decision.

## Fidelity Guardrails

Do not manufacture the following:

- a new “content framework” that replaces Hook–Retain–Reward;
- a mandatory decision stage the source does not name;
- one universal give-to-ask ratio;
- a claim that every piece needs an ask;
- a claim that every piece must contain a personal story;
- a fixed list, steps, or story requirement beyond retention needs;
- a universal publishing volume from the source's examples;
- a claim that audience growth proves commercial validation;
- a platform law from a self-reported manual-posting result;
- a guaranteed outcome from any source story, survey, or growth result.
