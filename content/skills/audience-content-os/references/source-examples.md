# Audience Content Source Examples

## How to Use This Bank

These examples come from the two source chapters on free content. Use them to understand how a rule operates. Do not copy their personal facts, results, audience, or offers into the user's work.

For each example:

1. identify the source rule it demonstrates;
2. transfer the mechanism, not the wording;
3. replace the facts only with verified facts belonging to the user;
4. keep an example labeled as an example rather than turning it into a mandatory template.

## One Small Content Unit

### The “no time” story

The source demonstrates Hook–Retain–Reward with a short personal story:

- **Hook:** the speaker complains to a friend about lacking time while using a phone.
- **Retain:** the friend takes the phone and checks its usage.
- **Reward:** the usage shows three hours per day on social media, revealing where the missing time went.

Why it works: the struggle is familiar, the event creates a natural “what did the friend find?” question, and the ending delivers a useful realization for people building businesses.

Transfer: find a real moment where an observed fact broke one of the user's beliefs. Do not reuse the phone story unless it happened to the user.

## Topic Examples

### Far Past

The “no time” story is a Far Past topic: an earlier lesson connected to the audience and business. The source describes this as giving people the lesson without requiring them to suffer the same consequence.

### Recent Past

The source points to a meeting with a portfolio-company CEO who copied the same offer as competitors and received weak results. The meeting becomes content because it contains a useful lesson tied to the work.

It also treats these as Recent Past raw material:

- last week's calendar;
- meetings and social interactions;
- warm-outreach conversations;
- notes, recordings, and other records;
- a client story, testimonial, or case study that teaches something while promoting the service.

Transfer: use the user's actual recent work and records. A client outcome is not required; a decision, mistake, observation, or mechanism can be the useful material.

### Present

The source describes capturing ideas during meetings by note, text, or email rather than trusting memory. It also describes publishing ideas as they occur and making more content when one topic performs above normal.

Transfer: use a real captured idea and the user's actual performance baseline. Do not invent an “outperformer.”

### Trending

The source gives no fixed trend template. Its example logic is simply to enter existing attention only when the creator's experience or expertise is relevant.

Transfer: explain the user's relevant connection before using the trend. Attention alone is insufficient.

### Manufactured

The source's example is deliberately living on a very small budget for a month, then showing how it was done. The creator makes the experience rather than documenting one that already occurred.

Transfer: propose something the user can genuinely learn, build, test, or do. Manufactured content costs more but can engineer a larger reward. For most entrepreneurs, the source still prefers sustainable documentation at higher volume.

## Headline Component Examples

The source uses news-like ingredients instead of fill-in-the-blank headline templates.

| Component | Source example logic | Transfer question |
|---|---|---|
| Recency | An event from an hour ago attracts more attention than one from a year ago. | Is the timing genuinely new or newly relevant? |
| Relevancy | Nurses care more about material affecting nurses than material affecting accountants. | Does this directly affect the intended audience? |
| Celebrity or authority | Ordinary breakfast choices become interesting when attached to a prominent business figure. | Is there a genuinely relevant recognizable person or authority? |
| Proximity | A nearby house fire matters more than one across the country. | Can the topic be truthfully brought closer to the audience's location or situation? |
| Conflict | Competing ideas such as pineapple versus no pineapple create tension. | Is there a real opposition in the material? |
| Unusualness | A rare physical characteristic attracts attention because it is outside the norm. | What is genuinely rare or unexpected here? |
| Ongoing development | People want frequent updates during an uncertain event such as labor. | Is the story truly unresolved and changing? |

Important: these are ingredients, not seven boxes to fill. The source suggests trying at least two, while its action line says one or more. It does not say every headline needs conflict, unusualness, a number, or a complete summary of every section.

### Format visual

The source places several images side by side to show that the same broad medium can have very different visual conventions. Its point is not to copy one image. The platform and target audience determine the expected look and feel.

Transfer: first make the content look native to the platform, then adapt it to the ideal audience using strong content in that market as the guide.

## Retention Examples

### Lists

The source gives examples such as:

- seven ways the creator invested $1,000 in his twenties that paid off;
- “28 Ways To Stay Poor”;
- announcing that a content unit has three pieces.

The number creates an unresolved question: what are all the items? The list must have a theme, but its items do not need an action order.

### Steps

The source gives examples such as:

- three steps for creating a hook;
- how the creator makes a headline in seven steps;
- a morning routine that produces a stated benefit.

The open question is how to complete the sequence and reach the result. These qualify as steps only when the order matters.

The fact that the source uses “How I Create a Headline in 7 Steps” as a generic example does not mean these chapters teach a specific seven-step headline method. Do not manufacture those seven steps.

### Stories

The source points to:

- stories opening many chapters;
- an editor requiring nineteen drafts and the question of what happened next;
- a journey from sleeping on a gym floor to staying at a five-star hotel.

The event sequence carries curiosity. The ending should deliver a lesson or takeaway, not merely an anecdote.

### Interwoven structures

The chapter itself combines lists inside steps and stories around them. It suggests stories for list items, stories reinforcing steps, lists of stories, and multiple ongoing storylines.

Transfer: combine structures only when each makes the value clearer or curiosity stronger.

## Reward Failure Examples

### Broken-number promise

A headline promises seven ways to repair a relationship, but the content delivers only four. The hook is not satisfied.

### Weak or familiar delivery

The piece provides all seven items, but they are poor or already obvious. Numerical completion alone does not create reward.

### Wrong audience

Relationship advice is delivered to people without spouses. Even potentially good advice fails when the audience cannot use it.

### Unusable professional advice

A piece promises marketing strategies dentists can use, but the strategies are inaccessible to dentists. The content does not reward the people it hooked.

Transfer: deliver the promised quantity, quality, relevance, and usability. The audience decides whether the reward worked.

## Short and Long Content Examples

The source treats one step in a five-step list as a possible content unit. Linking all five creates longer content. Each unit must hook, retain, and reward; a long piece requires more good units in sequence.

It compares a new comedian's short stage time with a master comic sustaining an hour, and epic novelists first learning through short stories. The lesson is to practice smaller units even when producing long work.

Transfer to an article: the title and opening create the initial reason to consume. Each section can then create and resolve another question. The title need not list every supporting unit, but the article must satisfy the main reason the title created.

## Give–Ask Examples

### Goodwill ratio examples

The source uses approximate mature-platform examples:

- television: about 47 minutes of content and 13 minutes of advertising;
- a social feed: roughly four content posts for one advertisement.

It treats these as monetization floors from mature platforms, not growth targets. Growing platforms give much more and ask much less.

### Give until they ask

The source describes people reciprocating by visiting a website, sending a direct message, or emailing. The preferred model is to give publicly and let people self-select privately.

### Integrated ask

The source's podcast example places three thirty-second advertisements inside an hour, leaving roughly 58.5 minutes of giving and 1.5 minutes of asking. This shows how an ask can appear in every long piece while the overall value remains high.

The counterexample is a fast-growing podcast that asked too frequently and reportedly shrank. The lesson is to protect goodwill and monitor growth, not to infer a universal numeric limit.

### Intermittent ask

The source illustrates ten pure-give posts followed by an eleventh promotional post. This explains the pattern; it is not a mandatory ten-to-one cadence.

### Lead-magnet ask

After giving one lead-generation tip, the source offers eleven more in a polished visual. The opt-in thank-you page then presents a video explaining the paid offer. The magnet works as a relevant extension of the content.

### Direct-offer ask

The source's example calls out a small number of a specific buyer, promises a desired outcome within a time frame without a stated sacrifice, provides refund-and-continued-work risk reversal, explains the confidence behind it, and offers a response path.

Transfer only real offer facts. Do not copy the number of buyers, result, time frame, effort claim, or guarantee.

### Review ask

The chapter's review interlude:

- identifies another entrepreneur as the beneficiary;
- asks for a review rather than money;
- frames the action as free and quick;
- explains why the action matters;
- gives instructions for different reading platforms;
- expresses gratitude and returns to value.

This demonstrates an ask. It does not establish a mandatory review-request formula.

## Distribution Examples

### Depth then width

The source sequence is: relevant platform, regular content, maximum quality and quantity, add another platform while maintaining the first, repeat. It cites up to ten short pieces per platform per day and up to five long-form days per week as possible maximums, not universal requirements.

### Width then depth

The source sequence is: publish regularly on one relevant platform, add each remaining relevant platform while maintaining earlier ones, then maximize all. A short video adapted with small formatting changes across short-video platforms illustrates efficient repurposing.

### Batch example

The source describes roughly one hour finding topics, twice-monthly sessions producing around thirty short clips, and two to four longer videos on the same day expanding written ideas with stories and examples.

Transfer the batching principle, not the exact calendar or quota.

## Seven-Lesson Examples

### “How I” instead of “how to”

The source contrasts:

- describing how the creator makes oatmeal versus telling everyone how they should make it;
- “How I Built My 7-Figure Agency” versus universal instructions for building one;
- the creator's favorite lead-generation method versus declaring one method objectively best.

Transfer: use experiential framing only when the user's experience supports it.

### Reminders instead of constant novelty

The source reports mentioning a book daily while one in five surveyed audience members still did not know it existed. The example supports repeating useful messages; it does not prove the same percentage for another audience.

### Puddles, ponds, lakes, oceans

A local plumber begins with plumbing in one town, then can expand toward local businesses, physical-location chains, and eventually general business. The example shows how narrow relevance can precede broad authority.

### Content as a sales tool

Create a master list of high-performing pieces. Label each by the problem it solves and benefit it provides, then let salespeople send the matched piece before or after calls, especially for recurring concerns.

### Free content and paying customers

Customers are more likely to consume free content and may include its quality in their perceived return from the paid product. Good free material can increase loyalty; bad free material can reduce perceived value.

### Higher standards, not shorter attention

The source points to people bingeing long series and long books. The issue is the availability of more rewarding alternatives, not a universal inability to focus.

### Manual publishing test

The source reports better results from manually submitted posts and theorizes that immediate feedback pressure improves the final quality. It says to try it. Treat this as a testable observation, not a platform law.

## Measurement Examples

### Absolute and relative growth

- 1,000 followers growing to 1,500 equals 500 additional followers and 50 percent growth.
- Reach growing from 10,000 to 15,000 equals 5,000 additional people and 50 percent growth.
- Ten listeners growing to fifteen is only five additional listeners but also 50 percent growth.

### Consistent input example

The source reports publishing a podcast twice weekly for four years before reaching a top-100 list, then reaching a top-ten United States business ranking and a top-500 global ranking in year five. This demonstrates consistency and learning time; it does not promise the same timeline or ranking.

## Why Content Supports Other Acquisition

The source reports that paid advertising weakened after gym content stopped. A survey reportedly found that 78 percent of clients had consumed at least two long-form pieces before booking. The source concludes that content nurtured demand and warmed people entering from or returning to cold methods.

Use this as the source's example and assertion. Do not claim the same attribution for the user's business without first-party evidence.

## First-Post Example

The source reproduces its first business post and immediately says it is not ideal and probably should not be copied. The useful lesson is to publish despite irrelevant opinions, give for a while if the first ask produces no engaged lead, and honor the people who believe in the creator through continued action.

Transfer: do not treat an old post as a model merely because it appears in the chapter. Preserve the source's own warning about the example.
