# $100M Leads Lead Magnet Source Framework

## Source Boundary

Primary source:

- Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads: How to Get Strangers to Want to Buy Your Stuff* (2023)
- Chapter: “Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets”
- PDF pages 44–72

Use this reference to preserve the source's operating logic. Do not add generic lead-generation conventions and attribute them to Hormozi.

## Core Thesis

A lead magnet is a free or lower-cost offer that completely solves one narrow problem for a specific customer. Solving that problem reveals another problem the core offer solves.

The customer's time is a price. A free magnet that is not worth consuming is overpriced.

The intended chain is:

```text
specific customer
  -> narrow meaningful problem P1
  -> complete lead-magnet solution S1
  -> next problem P2 becomes visible
  -> paid core-offer solution S2
```

The direct core offer can sometimes create enough engagement on its own. The chapter says to try that path first, but it does not make “decide whether you need a lead magnet” one of the seven creation steps. The creation sequence starts with the problem and customer.

## Opening Case Evidence

Hormozi describes:

- building and advertising a webinar;
- spending $450;
- generating 80 leads;
- producing no sales because people did not consume the webinar;
- replacing it with a simple 13-minute case-study walkthrough;
- changing the headline to a concrete gym result;
- filling the sales calendar with calls.

The lesson is not “webinars do not work.” An author note says webinars worked for others and that he lacked the skills to make one work at the time. The operational lesson is:

- give people something they want;
- test the offer instead of perfecting an untested product;
- a simple, wanted case study can outperform a more elaborate unwanted asset.

## The Four Outcome Tests

A good lead magnet:

1. engages ideal customers when they see it;
2. gets more people to engage than the core offer alone;
3. is valuable enough that they consume it;
4. makes the right people more likely to buy.

Do not treat lead volume, downloads, or email capture alone as success.

## The Seven Steps

### Step 1: Problem and Customer

Use the problem-solution cycle:

- every problem has a solution;
- every solution reveals more problems;
- smaller problem-solution cycles sit inside larger ones.

Pick a narrow and meaningful problem. Solve it. Confirm the new problem revealed by the solution is one the core offer can solve.

The real-estate example breaks the broad goal “sell a home” into preceding problems such as valuation, increasing value, photography, cleaning, landscaping, repairs, moving, and staging. Any one can become the narrow lead-magnet problem if solving it makes the remaining paid problem clearer.

### Step 2: Solution Type

Explore three types:

1. **Reveal a problem**
   - Diagnose an unknown gap, loss, risk, or worsening condition.
   - Connect the current state to the desired state and consequences.

2. **Sample or trial**
   - Provide full but limited access to the core solution.
   - Limit time, number of uses, or both.
   - Fit recurring solutions to recurring problems.

3. **One step in a multi-step process**
   - Complete one valuable step.
   - Sell the remaining steps or implementation.
   - Fit complex problems and services.

These types can be combined. The chapter's dressing-room callout shows one experience can:

- reveal a problem when the customer tries the product;
- provide a sample of the solution;
- complete one step in a larger process.

Another callout argues for giving the strongest useful value first. Do not dilute the magnet because it is free.

### Step 3: Delivery Mechanism

Translate the solution through:

1. **Software:** spreadsheet, calculator, dashboard, or small tool that performs work.
2. **Information:** course, lesson, expert interview, presentation, event, mistakes, pitfalls, tips, or other teaching.
3. **Service:** perform useful work for free or at lower cost.
4. **Physical product:** deliver a tangible item.

Three types multiplied by four mechanisms create twelve starting combinations. Generate and rotate multiple versions to keep promotion fresh and discover what works.

### Step 4: Name and Display

Test:

1. headline;
2. image;
3. subheadline.

The headline receives priority. The chapter demonstrates that small word changes changed poll preferences materially.

Testing paths include:

- audience polls;
- posts asking for a binary response;
- direct messages;
- controlled promotional tests.

A strong signal appears when respondents ask when they can obtain the magnet.

### Step 5: Consumption

Reduce effort and widen access:

- make software accessible from convenient devices and paths;
- offer information as useful combinations of image, video, text, and audio;
- make services available across convenient times and channels;
- simplify physical ordering, delivery, opening, instructions, and use.

The source explicitly recommends multiple consumption formats. Do not impose a one-format rule.

### Step 6: Quality

Give away the method or secrets and sell implementation.

The magnet should:

- provide real value before purchase;
- be worth the prospect's time;
- solve P1 completely;
- be as reputation-worthy as paid work;
- be valuable enough that charging for it would be reasonable;
- make the likely value of the paid offer easier to believe.

The chapter argues that weak free material harms reputation even among the majority who do not buy.

### Step 7: CTA

Place the CTA after consumption.

Include:

1. what to do;
2. a reason to do it now.

Reason categories:

- **scarcity:** advertise real inventory, onboarding, service, capacity, or scheduling limits;
- **urgency:** use a genuine end time, expiring promotion, or time-limited bonus;
- **because:** give a reason rather than no reason.

The chapter's preferred urgency tactic is to time-limit bonuses and rotate them, allowing urgency without capping overall sales. Apply this only with truthful dates, terms, and availability.

## Economics

The source allows delivery cost when it produces more value and lowers customer acquisition cost.

Illustrative book example:

- Core-offer path:
  - $1,000 advertising cost per call;
  - one customer per three calls;
  - $3,000 acquisition cost.

- Lead-magnet path:
  - $75 advertising plus $25 delivery per call;
  - $100 total per call;
  - one customer per ten recipients;
  - $1,000 acquisition cost.

This is an illustration, not a benchmark.

Use:

```text
total cost per engaged lead
  = advertising cost per engaged lead
  + delivery cost per engaged lead

customer acquisition cost
  = total lead-magnet path cost
  / customers acquired
```

Compare the lead-magnet path with the business's actual direct-offer baseline.

## Source-Faithful Guardrails

Do not:

- define a lead magnet as an email-gated download;
- start with a format before identifying P1 and the customer;
- make “decide whether you need one” a build stage;
- treat the three solution types as mutually exclusive;
- require one consumption format;
- invent an arbitrary ideal length or consumption time;
- import buyer-stage matrices, gating rules, form benchmarks, or channel benchmarks without another named source;
- optimize downloads while ignoring consumption, buyer fit, and purchase likelihood;
- withhold the value required to solve P1;
- add a CTA before value is delivered;
- fabricate scarcity, urgency, proof, or results;
- present the illustrative acquisition math as a universal result.

## Distillation Status

- **Core thesis:** Solve one narrow problem completely, reveal the next paid problem, and maximize engagement through packaging, consumption, value, and a clear CTA.
- **Evidence strength:** Medium. The chapter contains concrete author case evidence and operating examples, but it is one primary source rather than independent comparative research.
- **Reuse potential:** High. The workflow is reusable and its outputs are testable.
- **Promotion decision:** Promote for source-grounded execution because the user explicitly requested the skill and the source includes an execution case. Keep empirical claims labeled and validate results against BizOps campaign data.
