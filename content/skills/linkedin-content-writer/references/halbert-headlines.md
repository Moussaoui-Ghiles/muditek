# Captured Gary Halbert writing reference

This file covers the complete captured corpus in library/sources/web/gary-halbert: 19 source notes. It covers this captured corpus, not everything Gary Halbert published.

## What Halbert teaches

Halbert places the headline inside AIDA. Its job is attention. The opening that follows must earn interest instead of letting the headline's attention die.

His headline rules are direct:

1. Calling the reader by name is the strongest attention device when real personalization is possible and the user explicitly requests it.
2. When it is not, put news in the headline, promise a benefit, or combine both.
3. Make the headline true and relevant to the reader.
4. Prefer specific facts over general claims. His worked teardown shows how a precise authority, discovery, benefit, and timeframe make a claim clearer, more believable, and more interesting.
5. Audit the headline phrase by phrase. Every phrase must perform a job.
6. Write headlines to improve at headlines. Study proven headlines, review what already worked, generate alternatives, and use playful options to find a usable idea.

## What the "Killer Headlines" letter actually demonstrates

The letter does not teach one headline formula. It demonstrates this process:

1. Get attention by addressing the reader directly when real personalization is available.
2. Otherwise lead with news, a benefit, or both.
3. Make the claim true and relevant.
4. Replace general claims with concrete authority, action, benefit, number, or timeframe.
5. Review proven headlines before drafting.
6. Write several directions, including deliberately playful ones, to find the useful idea.
7. Return to the source facts and select the strongest truthful direction.
8. Inspect the selected headline phrase by phrase.

Its headline starters include: Announcing; At last; Now; How to; Here are; a numbered set of ways; the art of; the secret of; a startling fact about; amazing; and new. These are starting moves, not words to force into every headline.

The letter's central worked example combines news, authority, discovery, novelty, a common desire, and a precise timeframe:

> At Last! Scientists Discover New Way To Look Younger In Just 17-Days!

Halbert then explains the job of each phrase. The lesson is not to copy the wording. Build a line whose parts each add attention, credibility, benefit, specificity, or curiosity.

The letter also demonstrates these headline shapes. Replace every bracket with an exact source fact. Use no shape the source cannot support.

- `[Approved reader name] + [surprising event or consequence]`. Use only when the user explicitly requests the name.
- `WARNING: Do not [expected action] until you learn [important fact].`
- `[Specific offer] on [scope] by [deadline] because [real event].`
- `Need [desired outcome]? [Number] reasons not to [expected choice].`
- `[Specific offer or result] may cause [credible conflict or consequence].`
- `Who else wants [specific outcome] without [specific cost or delay] for [timeframe]?`
- `Do you make these [category] mistakes when [common action]?`
- `Are you willing to [small cost] to gain or save [quantified benefit]?`
- `[Seller] denies [suspicious explanation] for [unusually strong offer].`
- `The surprising secret behind [specific unusual action].`
- State a concrete result, then state the starting baseline as the proof subhead.

Halbert separates practice from publication. The bizarre practice headlines are used to loosen the writer's thinking. The final headline must return to what is true, relevant, and supported by the source.

Halbert does not stop at the headline. His AIDA letter shows the next paragraph opening a story, question, claim, physical demonstration, confession, or surprising fact. It then gives concrete details and moves into the promised value.

## LinkedIn application

For each source:

1. State the exact subject.
2. Find the strongest truthful news, benefit, or combination already present.
3. Add the source's best specificity: number, timeframe, mechanism, event, or visible result. Use a name only when the user explicitly requests it.
4. Draft alternatives using only the source.
5. Audit the strongest alternatives phrase by phrase.
6. Select the headline that gives the clearest reason to consume and that the body can fulfil.
7. Follow it with concrete source-backed lines that create the next question or begin the story.

The captured letters also use direct promises, questions, contradiction, stories in motion, demonstrations, named frameworks, specific proof, and exact numbered promises. Use the form that fits the source.

## Keeping attention

Halbert carries attention with worked examples, dialogue, proof, escalation, repeated refrains, demonstrations, physical artifacts, numbered progress, and delayed reveals. These devices work because each one advances the question opened earlier.

## Paying off attention

Deliver the named items, full sequence, resolution, proof, answer, or conclusion. Exact promises require exact delivery. Strong closes return to the opening, land the lesson through evidence, provide the finished artifact, or place one additional action in a P.S.
