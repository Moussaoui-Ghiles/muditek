## Specificity — the single most important page

Hopkins ch. 7: "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." A superlative teaches the reader to discount everything else you write. A specific claim is believed because **"a man who makes a specific claim is either telling the truth or a lie."**

His own tested examples. These are the standard:

| Dead | Alive |
|---|---|
| gives more light | gives three and one-third times the light |
| our prices have been reduced | our prices have been reduced 25 per cent |
| lowest prices in America | our net profit is 3 per cent |
| abundant lather | multiplies itself in lather 250 times |
| a quick shave | a 78-second shave |
| pure beer | 1,018 experiments to fix the yeast, water from 4,000 feet down, bottles washed four times |
| high food value | 425 calories per pint, equal to six eggs |
| used the world over | used by the peoples of 52 nations |

The shaving soap entered a saturated market where every customer had to be taken from a competitor, and won on those four numbers alone.

**Every adjective is a candidate for deletion and replacement by a fact.** No fact available, cut the sentence.

**Attribute the number, never the research process** (Halbert, 8 of 19 letters, the second most frequent mechanic in the corpus). A figure carries whose figure it is - an operator, a company, or a study - because an orphaned number reads as the author's result. It does not need a sentence about where the author found it. "One production store logged 10,000 entries in a month" is clear. "An article I read cites a store that logged 10,000" narrates research instead of teaching.

**Voice doubt about your own best statistic.** Halbert states an impressive number then asks openly whether it can really be true. Reads as more credible than clean confidence. Cheap to execute.

## Sentence law

**Write to one person.** Hopkins ch. 2: "Don't think of people in the mass. That gives you a blurred view." "Founders" is the mass. "You, with two closers and nothing on their calendar" is one person.

**Fine writing is a liability.** Hopkins ch. 2, exactly: "Fine writing is a distinct disadvantage. So is unique literary style. They take attention from the subject. **They reveal the hook.**" A polished literary voice reads like an over-smooth salesman and creates resistance.

**Clarity outranks entertainment.** Story, tension, surprise, and personality are useful when they make the problem or mechanism easier to understand. Ornament that competes with the subject is cut.

**Fifth grade.** Short words, short sentences, one thought each. Shorter word wins. Georgi's own checklist puts it as: reads like a friend talking, not like prose.

**One vocabulary for the whole piece.** Pick the single word for the unit of work and use only that word. The 2026-07-28 draft ran lanes, layers, assets, brain, notes, files, briefs and rulings at once, all describing overlapping things, and each section renamed the subject before the reader had anything to stand on. When inputs disagree on vocabulary, choose one and translate the others into it.

**Prefer one anchoring example when it can carry the whole argument.** Use additional examples only when they prove a different condition or limitation. Do not force one example into sections it cannot explain.

**Never lift a term that only means something inside the source.** "If it is not recorded, it did not happen to your intelligence" is a real line from a real talk where "your intelligence" meant the AI system. In a letter it refers to nothing. Say the plain version instead.

**Subheads are never first person.** A subhead written as the reader speaking ("Which lane do I hand over first", "What has to wait for me") flips the narrator at every section break in a first-person piece. Write them as plain statements of what the section covers.

**Never print a template with its placeholders as prose.** "The problem was costing me the pain, I discovered the solution, and now the outcome" is a fill-in-the-blank exercise, not a sentence. Either fill it with the input's real example or describe the exercise.

**Complete thoughts, never stacked fragments.** "Five subscriptions. Revenue flat. Wrong structure." hands the reader three pieces and asks them to build the claim. They will not. Write the claim: "Five subscriptions did not move your revenue because the structure was wrong." Same facts, one read. This governs titles, subheads and body equally.

**A quotable central claim may anchor the issue.** Repeat it only when the repetition adds orientation; never repeat it to imitate a sales letter.

**State the rule, attach one reason, stop** (Halbert). Do not build the reasoning out further.

**Use a concrete metaphor only when it clarifies the literal rule.** The literal explanation must still stand on its own.

**Same voice every issue.** Hopkins ch. 17: "People do not know us by name alone, but by looks and mannerisms... a change in our characteristics would compel our best friends to get acquainted all over."

**Positive framing.** Hopkins ch. 18 ran the direct test: "Send now for this sample" beat "Why do you neglect this offer?" **four to one.** Never blame the reader for not having acted. Never attack a named competitor.

**No manufactured urgency, deadlines, scarcity, or pressure.** A real deadline, event, capacity limit, or cost of delay may be stated plainly when it is verified and relevant.

**No em-dashes. Anywhere.**

## Formatting

Long-form is **paragraphs of two to four sentences.** That is the default and most of the piece.

A line standing alone is **emphasis.** Three or four times in an entire issue, on the lines carrying the most weight. Spent on every sentence it means nothing.

> [!danger] Banned: the one-sentence-per-line wall
> A whole piece broken one sentence per line is a **feed-post device.** In long-form it reads like a WhatsApp thread and it destroys the argument, because nothing can be emphasized when everything is.
> Current negative example: the Hormozi newsletter, which now breaks after every sentence. Do not imitate it.

- **Subheads orient the reader.** Use a plain statement or a real reader question, keep the narrator consistent, and never use generic labels such as "Introduction."
- **Lists earn their place.** For genuinely parallel items. Prose chopped into bullets is worse than the prose was.
- **Bold carries the load-bearing sentence** in a section, inline in a paragraph. One per section at most. Bold is never used for a subhead — that is what the heading level is for.
- **No emojis. No hashtags.**

## Kill list

Cut on sight:

- "In today's world", "let's dive in", "the reality is", "here's the thing", "hope this helps"
- Announcing structure: "first, let's look at", "now that we've covered"
- Hedges: "arguably", "somewhat", "it could be said"
- Recapping what was just said
- Any sentence that survives being deleted

---

## Final read

Read it as someone who has never seen the source and does not know the author. Both must pass:

1. Can they act on it without clicking anything?
2. Does every line make the next line worth reading?
