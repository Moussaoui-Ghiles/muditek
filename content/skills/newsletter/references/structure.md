## Rule zero

**Every sentence creates clarity or it is cut.** A sentence the reader has to assemble, re-read, or decode has failed, no matter how true it is. This outranks every other rule in this file.

---

## Pick one of two shapes

The corpus contains exactly two, and no edition mixes them.

### Shape A — the essay

Observed in Justin Welsh's current era, 12 of 12 editions. Use when the payoff is a way of seeing something.

1. **Cold open on a specific real scene or operating situation.** No greeting, preamble, or throat-clearing. A public case may name its people. A private case may be truthfully anonymized when permission requires it. Use only verified details.
2. **One idea, developed across one to three headed sections.** Never more than three. Several editions use none at all and run as one continuous thread.
3. **A second case making the same point.** Half the essays do this: a contrast figure, a repeat of the same criticism years later, a named counter-example.
4. **A callback to the opening scene**, landing on something concrete from it. The nickel. The two people from the news story.
5. **A callback or final judgment, followed by the approved close when one exists.**

### Shape B — the guide

Observed in Welsh's 2022 tactical era, all 7 ColdIQ editions, and most of Lenny's. Use when the payoff is a procedure the reader runs.

1. **The promise or a verified quantified claim, in sentence one.** Do not invent a number because the shape often uses one.
2. **One or two sentences stating the method or the roadmap.** What this piece will walk through.
3. **Numbered sections, one step each,** every one carrying a real named example. ColdIQ names the providers. Welsh names the creators. Lenny names the companies.
4. **A recap.** Welsh closes on a TL;DR block. ColdIQ closes on an FAQ whose first answer restates the entire piece in one paragraph. Pick one.
5. **The approved close or CTA.**

## Storytelling law

A story must come from a verified public case, an approved first-party artifact, or the user in the current task.

- Name public people and companies when attribution strengthens proof.
- Anonymize private client or buyer stories when permission requires it.
- Never invent a person, scene, quote, date, number, feeling, or physical detail.
- Use only the details required to make the decision and mechanism concrete.

---

## Every edition ends deliberately

The corpus often uses an ask, but BizOps does not force one. End with one of:

- a callback or final judgment;
- a practical reader action;
- one relevant question;
- one matched CTA when the content job is conversion.

Use at most one ask. It must flow from what was taught.

Closing moves that recur: the callback to the opening scene, landing on something concrete. A single imperative ("start swinging", "open Claude and ask your next question"). A stated decision rule the reader can carry.

---

## Length

Welsh's tactical era ran 200 to 900 words and grew the list past 200,000. His essays run longer but stay a single thread. ColdIQ and Lenny run long, and subhead every few hundred words.

Length follows the shape. A guide with nine real steps is long. An essay on one idea is not.

---

## When there is no scene

Shape A needs a real scene or operating situation and there will not always be one. In that case use Shape B, which needs no anecdote and may open on a clear promise.

Never invent a scene. Never use a private client story without permission. Truthful anonymization is allowed when it protects a real person or company; invented vagueness is not.
